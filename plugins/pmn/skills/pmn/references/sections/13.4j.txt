PMN Framework v126 | Part XIII: Tensions That Cannot Be Resolved — Only Managed
§13.4j When All Available Options Produce Structural Suffering: The Structural Tragedy Problem
Source: https://novadharma-hub.github.io/pmn-framework/read/13.4j.html

A distinct class of limitation requires explicit acknowledgment: situations in which every available institutional option produces structural suffering on the framework’s own criterion, and no option exists that reduces structural suffering for one population without increasing it for another. The framework’s standard prescriptions — identify the arrangement producing unnecessary suffering, specify the transformation that would reduce it, evaluate the institutional changes required — assume that a better arrangement is available, even if reaching it is difficult. The structural tragedy problem arises when that assumption fails: when the genuine constraint is not the difficulty of reaching a better arrangement but the absence of one.

The framework resists this framing as a starting point, because its most common use is to justify arrangements that do produce unnecessary suffering by asserting, without adequate analysis, that no alternative exists. This is the characteristic move of conservative institutional defense: naturalizing contingent arrangements as necessary by presenting alternatives as impossible. The framework’s default position — that visible structural suffering almost always has a structural cause and almost always has an alternative arrangement that would reduce it — is analytically warranted by the historical record, which shows that arrangements previously presented as necessary alternatives have, under pressure, proved contingent and changeable. Any analysis that invokes the structural tragedy framing without first completing the full alternatives assessment has invoked a conclusion that requires rigorous prior demonstration.

The conditions under which the structural tragedy framing is analytically legitimate: the claim that all available options produce structural suffering must survive the stress-test methodology in 12.1 — specifically, the reversal test (does the claim hold when applied symmetrically to the actors who benefit from the current arrangement?), the opposing constituency test (have the alternatives been assessed from the perspective of those who bear the costs of the arrangement being defended?), and the historical test (have comparable configurations in comparable historical conditions produced genuine transformations that the current analysis treats as unavailable?). An analysis that has completed all three tests and still concludes that no option avoids structural suffering has met the evidentiary standard. An analysis that skips any of the three has not. The conclusion must additionally be verified by actors who do not benefit from it — precisely because the Custodian Problem in 7.3 applies to tragic choice declarations with particular force: an actor who stands to benefit from accepting a specific harmful arrangement as necessary has both the incentive and the institutional capacity to assess alternatives in ways that systematically undercount them.

When the structural tragedy conclusion survives evidentiary scrutiny, the framework’s analytical function shifts from identifying the arrangement that reduces suffering to specifying what the constrained decision requires. Four demands follow. First, the specific populations bearing the costs and the specific populations bearing the benefits must be named explicitly — no tragedy framing is analytically complete without this specification. Second, the distribution requirement in 8.3 applies with heightened force: a tragic choice that systematically places costs on already-structurally-disadvantaged populations while placing benefits on already-advantaged ones fails the consistency standard regardless of the aggregate calculation. Third, the decision must include explicit provisions for restoring conditions for those who bear the costs — not as a compensatory afterthought, but as a structural commitment undertaken before the decision is implemented. Fourth, the decision is provisional: it is the best available option given current conditions, not a settled institutional arrangement, and must be monitored for the appearance of alternatives the current analysis did not identify.

What the framework cannot provide: it cannot identify which specific decision should be made in a genuine structural tragedy. That requires detailed empirical knowledge of specific conditions that the framework, as a general analytical tool, does not possess. What it can do is distinguish genuine structural tragedy from the use of tragedy framing to exempt harmful arrangements from scrutiny, and specify the analytical and procedural conditions under which a constrained choice can be made with minimum additional harm beyond what the constraint itself requires.
