PMN Framework v126 | Part XII: How This Framework Operates
§12.1c Extreme Scenario Testing as Assumption Archaeology
Source: https://novadharma-hub.github.io/pmn-framework/read/12.1c.html

The stress-testing methodology in 12.1 operates by testing claims against adversarial realistic cases — historically documented counter-examples, plausible opposing constituencies, structurally similar situations with different outcomes. This is the appropriate procedure for evaluating whether structural claims hold under the conditions for which they were developed. A complementary procedure serves a different function: the use of deliberately extreme scenarios — configurations that are not realistic predictions and are not intended to be — to identify assumptions embedded in the framework that remain invisible when applied to realistic cases.

The mechanism by which hidden assumptions become visible in extreme scenarios is structural. When a scenario pushes variables to limits that realistic cases never reach — when resource deprivation is absolute rather than severe, when institutional legitimacy is zero rather than low, when adaptive response capacity is fully eliminated rather than constrained — the conclusions the framework generates depend on assumptions that carry no weight under realistic conditions but become load-bearing at the extreme. The extreme scenario does not test whether the scenario is realistic. It tests which assumptions the framework’s conclusions actually rest on in conditions where those assumptions can no longer be taken for granted.

Four categories of extreme scenario are analytically productive for PMN, distinguished by the class of assumption they target.

Total institutional collapse. A scenario in which all institutional structures simultaneously lose both enforcement capacity and legitimacy — government, legal system, civil society, and economic institutions — leaving no custodial authority capable of response. The analytical function: revealing whether the framework’s prescriptions for institutional design rest on the assumption of at least some surviving institutional infrastructure. In total collapse conditions, the framework’s prescriptions for reform, accountability, and graduated transformation do not apply in their standard form — because the institutional channels through which they operate are themselves the variable that has reached zero. This extreme is useful not because total institutional collapse is a realistic scenario but because it forces specification of what the framework’s prescriptions actually assume: not that institutions are adequate, but that institutions capable of transformation exist. Where that assumption fails partially — in fragile states, post-conflict societies, settings with institutional capacity far below what reform prescriptions implicitly require — the extreme scenario’s diagnostic function applies in attenuated form.

Enforced behavioral modification at population scale. A scenario in which the state has both the capacity and the declared intention to mandate specific behavioral or biological changes across an entire population — including changes to reproduction, cognition, or identity — on the grounds that the modification reduces structural suffering in aggregate. The analytical function: revealing the specific boundary at which the framework’s anti-paternalism principle activates in the face of aggregate-welfare arguments. The framework’s evaluative criterion is not utilitarian — it does not endorse aggregate optimization at the cost of distributional harm to specific populations — but the specific mechanism through which aggregate arguments are blocked, and the conditions under which population-level intervention can be justified, are more clearly specified by this extreme than by realistic cases where the trade-off is less stark.

AI-driven institutional capture at full penetration. A scenario in which the decision-making architecture of all major institutions has been delegated to or effectively controlled by automated systems that optimize for measurable proxies of institutional purpose rather than the purpose itself — and in which those systems cannot be modified by the populations they affect because they are not subject to the accountability mechanisms through which human actors can be reached. The analytical function: revealing whether the Custodian Problem in 7.3 as developed assumes a human actor in the custodian role — one who is subject to social pressure, reputational incentives, and physical vulnerability — or whether it applies with equal force to non-human custodians not subject to those accountability levers. This scenario is not hypothetical in its structural content: partial versions are present in algorithmic content curation, automated credit decisions, and predictive policing systems. The extreme forces specification of which elements of the accountability analysis require modification when the custodian cannot be removed, does not respond to legitimacy pressure, and cannot be held responsible through any existing institutional channel.

Post-scarcity with preserved developmental inequality. A scenario in which material needs are fully met for all populations — floor deprivation has been eliminated as a structural phenomenon — but developmental inequality remains extreme: the conditions for cognitive, relational, and political development are unevenly distributed, and a substantial portion of the population has adequate material conditions but no available path toward the forms of development the anti-foreclosure criterion is designed to protect. The analytical function: revealing whether the framework’s evaluative architecture has sufficient resources to evaluate institutional arrangements once the floor criterion is largely satisfied. The relevant discussion is in 13.4b; the extreme scenario is useful as a stress-test of whether the anti-foreclosure criterion generates actionable prescriptions in conditions where the floor criterion is not the binding constraint, and whether the transformation pressure analysis — calibrated primarily to floor-level suffering — has adequate tools for conditions where floor deprivation is absent but developmental inequality is high.

Analytical discipline for extreme scenario use: extreme scenarios are not policy planning exercises and should not be used as such. They are assumption archaeology — procedures for making visible what the framework’s conclusions rest on by pushing variables to points where those commitments become explicit. The appropriate output of an extreme scenario analysis is a specification of hidden assumptions, not a prescription for the scenario itself. An analysis that produces policy prescriptions from an extreme scenario has confused the scenario’s function: it has generated a predetermined answer for a situation that will not occur, without the scrutiny that realistic cases would supply. The scenarios above are analytically useful precisely because they are unrealistic. Their policy prescriptions, if produced, would be contaminated by the unrealism in ways that their assumption-revealing function is not.
